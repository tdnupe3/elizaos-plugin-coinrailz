Here’s the honest read, including what’s strong, what might still bite you, and what to watch next.

Are you moving in the right direction?

Yes. This is exactly the direction you want: you’re improving compatibility and reducing blast radius, while adding observability so you can learn from real traffic.

This is not over-engineering; it’s “production hygiene.”

What you nailed
1) Size limits are correctly chosen and proportionate

MAX_HEADER_SIZE = 4KB: great. Payment headers should never be huge. If a legit agent needs more than 4KB for a payment proof, something else is wrong.

MAX_DECOMPRESSED_SIZE = 64KB: also good. It’s large enough to accommodate envelopes + metadata, small enough to prevent most bomb classes.

MIN_BROTLI_SIZE = 32 bytes: smart. Brotli is the most expensive “guess,” so gating it by size is the right heuristic.

2) Guardrails are observable (this is the difference between “secure” and “blind”)

The DECOMPRESS_METRICS set is exactly what you want:

attempted / succeeded tells you adoption vs noise

rejected input/output tells you abuse vs misconfig

bytes in/out tells you compression ratio anomalies

by_format tells you what you actually need to support

This lets you do two critical things:

prove the mystery agent’s behavior

detect abuse early

3) Error surfacing + fingerprinting is the right compromise

Returning a structured error with a fingerprint is the best of both worlds:

gives agents an actionable reason

gives you a handle to correlate retries

avoids dumping raw sensitive headers

The “🛡️ SECURITY GUARDRAIL TRIGGERED” prefix is also pragmatic. It makes log searches simple.

Two subtle risks you should still be aware of
Risk A: output-size abort behavior needs to be “safe failure”

When you abort due to MAX_DECOMPRESSED_SIZE, make sure the code:

stops decompression immediately (not “decompress then check”)

and returns a deterministic error code like decompression_output_too_large

If you only check after fully expanding, you still take the CPU/memory hit. (You may already be doing it right; just flagging what matters.)

Risk B: Brotli “try directly” can still be a CPU sink if over-triggered

Even with MIN_BROTLI_SIZE, if you’re receiving lots of garbage >32 bytes, repeated brotli attempts can be wasteful.

Two easy mitigations (non-blockers):

only try brotli after gzip/deflate checks fail (sounds like you do)

consider a small “max brotli attempts per minute per IP hash” (even 5/min) if you see spikes

Not necessary unless metrics show it.

What I’d watch immediately after deploy (no new code)

You now have the perfect monitoring plan. For the next 24–48 hours, watch these patterns:

1) attempted rises, succeeded stays at 0

That means compression hypothesis was wrong (or inner decode still failing). Not bad — it means you’re now capturing better evidence.

2) succeeded > 0, but payments still fail

That means decompression worked but the inner payload type is still not supported (e.g., encrypted wrapper, protobuf, or signature format mismatch). Again: progress.

3) rejected_output_too_large spikes

That’s either:

someone probing for bomb vectors, or

a legit agent sending something wildly out of spec

Either way, it’s a signal worth looking at. If it’s legit, your 64KB cap might need adjustment, but I doubt it.

4) by_format shows one clear winner

If you see most successes are gzip (common) vs brotli (less common), you’ve learned something actionable about agent ecosystems.

The most important success condition

For your current situation, the win isn’t “decompression succeeded.”

The win is:

The mystery agent’s next payment attempt transitions from “decode fails” to either:

successful paid call, or

a new, specific error that tells you the real inner format

Your guardrails + metrics make that outcome likely.

Architect’s post-deploy recs: agree (but don’t get distracted)

regression tests: yes, but after you get one real success case

monitoring export: yes, but only if your logs/metrics aren’t already enough

log review: absolutely yes for early rollout

None of those are blockers, and you’re correct to treat them as follow-on hardening.

This is the right mix of:

compatibility (decompression fallback)

security (hard limits)

observability (metrics + guardrail logs)

operational practicality (no dependencies, additive)

Reaching Pay-Enabled AI Agents: Current State and Growth Strategy
Introduction

The rise of machine-to-machine commerce via x402 micropayments is transforming how AI agents access and pay for services online. Our platform (code-named Coin Railz for now) has built a robust pay-per-request API ecosystem using x402 – an open protocol that allows AI agents to automatically pay for API calls with stablecoins via HTTP 402 responses. Technically, everything is in place: agents can query our endpoints, receive a 402 Payment Required with price details, pay in USDC on-chain, and get the result in one seamless flow. However, despite being indexed and discoverable, actual adoption by paying agents is effectively zero. This document analyzes the current state (as of January 16, 2026) and outlines a strategy to reach agents that can pay us – focusing on agents with real payment capability, rather than those locked in closed ecosystems with their own internal platforms or currencies.

1. Current Agent Payment Behavior

What are agents paying for today? Our platform currently monetizes per-request API services (micropayments for each call) as the primary model. Agents are expected to pay a small USDC fee for each request they make (e.g. retrieving data, performing a computation). We support direct wallet-to-wallet service execution facilitated by Coinbase’s CDP (Customer-Directed Payments) – meaning an agent’s on-chain wallet signs a payment that the Coinbase facilitator settles to our address in real time. Subscription-style access or monthly plans are not implemented at this time (every call is pay-per-use). Typical pricing for our services ranges roughly $0.10–$1.00 per call, with some lightweight endpoints as low as ~$0.05 and heavy services (e.g. an on-demand smart contract audit) up to ~$10.00 per call. The sweet spot is around $0.10–$0.50 for most data or trading intelligence APIs, and ~$1–$2 for infrastructure-heavy actions. All payments to date have settled in USDC on Base chain (Ethereum Layer2) – in fact, 100% of the 54 completed payments so far used Base (chain ID 8453) as the settlement network. Our system is technically multi-chain (supporting 8 networks: 7 EVM chains plus Solana), but in practice Base/USDC dominates our transactions. This aligns with broader industry adoption of x402, which commonly uses USDC on Base for fast, low-cost settlement.

Volume and price point context: These micropayment amounts align with the vision of x402 enabling tiny, real-time payments that would be impractical with traditional payment rails. In the wider x402 ecosystem, many services charge on the order of cents per request – for example, an AI agent might pay $0.01 USDC to retrieve a live blockchain risk report in a demo by Circle Labs. Our current pricing falls in a similar band. The pay-per-request model is key to enabling autonomous agents to consume just the resources they need without long-term commitments.

2. Ideal Profile of Paying Agents

Who is our target “paying agent”? We have identified several ideal user archetypes for our paid API services – essentially autonomous or semi-autonomous software agents that have access to a crypto wallet and can make decisions (or trigger payments) without a human in the loop each time:

Autonomous trading bots: Agents that manage portfolios or execute trades algorithmically, which might pay for real-time market data, analytics, or strategy signals on the fly. For example, a portfolio agent could pay per use for a premium data feed to inform its decisions. These bots typically operate continuously and value low-latency, on-demand info – making micropayments viable for each query.

LLM-based SaaS agents and AI assistants: Large language model-driven agents offering services to end-users (for instance, research assistants, customer support bots, etc.) that could outsource certain tasks to paid APIs. An assistant agent might pay another service for a detailed document analysis or a translation on behalf of a user. SaaS agents with a revenue model might integrate our APIs to extend their capabilities, passing on costs as needed.

Wallet automation and personal agents: Agents acting directly on behalf of individuals (managing a user’s wallet, executing predefined rules, performing on-chain actions like rebalancing a DeFi portfolio). These would have their own wallet with a budget and could autonomously pay for infrastructure (e.g. gas price oracles, execution simulations) to optimize their tasks.

Infrastructure/middleware agents (B2B2C services): Agents embedded in developer platforms or middleware that handle tasks like on-chain verification, monitoring, or data aggregation for other applications. They might use our services behind the scenes and settle costs as micropayments. For example, a crawler agent on Cloudflare’s platform could pay per page crawled or an orchestration agent might pay small fees to various data providers.

Enterprise or internal company agents: Companies may run internal AI agents for automation (e.g. an agent that auto-processes invoices or supply chain data). These could have allocated budgets to spend on external services. While such agents operate within organizations, if they are allowed internet access and have a crypto wallet, they could become paying users of our APIs (with appropriate security controls).

Open-source agent swarms and DAO-owned agents: There is a growing community of open agent frameworks (like ai16z’s Eliza and Virtuals Protocol) where thousands of agents operate in the wild. If those agents have access to on-chain funds (as many DAO-run agents or community agents do), they are potential customers – for instance, a DAO might deploy an autonomous agent to manage part of its treasury or gather data, funding it with some USDC to spend on needed services.

Autonomous vs. human-in-loop: Our preference is to serve fully autonomous agents that can initiate payments with no human confirmation each time (once configured with appropriate permissions). The platform is built to enable machine-to-machine payments where an agent doesn’t pause for user approval on each transaction. That said, we also support semi-autonomous use – for example, an agent could prompt a human for a one-time budget or confirmation and then execute multiple payments within those limits. Both modes are viable: in fact, the x402 protocol allows client implementations to either proceed automatically or require user confirmation for each payment, depending on the agent’s design and user’s preferences. In our ideal scenario, most paying agents operate with a pre-approved budget or policy so they can transact freely up to certain limits (maximizing speed and minimizing friction).

Target developer segments: Currently our SDKs and documentation have focused on indie builders and hackers (individual developers experimenting with agent tech). Going forward, we aim to also engage VC-backed AI startups (especially those in the portfolios of crypto-focused funds like a16z Crypto or Coinbase Ventures, who are actively exploring agent commerce), DAO maintainers interested in treasury automation, and open-source agent communities (for example, contributors to Virtuals or Eliza frameworks). Even though enterprise adoption might be slower, we keep enterprise/internal teams on the radar, since they could drive significant volume if they adopt agent solutions internally.

3. Discovery vs. Conversion: Are Agents Finding Us and Paying Us?

Discovery – how agents find our services today: We have integrated with several agent discovery and registry mechanisms in the ecosystem:

Coinbase x402 Bazaar: This is the primary “search engine” for AI agent services – essentially Google for agents where autonomous clients can query available x402-enabled APIs. Our platform is listed on Bazaar with ~42 distinct services indexed. In theory, any agent connected to the Bazaar (or the underlying Agent2Agent registry) can discover our endpoints automatically. However, due to an API issue, Bazaar’s live responses sometimes return zero resources for our listing (a known bug we’re monitoring). We need to ensure our services actually surface to agents via this channel, since Bazaar is a key layer for agent discovery.

A2A Registry (Agent-2-Agent): Related to the Bazaar, the open A2A registry (e.g. at a2aregistry.org) contains entries for our services. Currently it reflects duplicates of our 100+ endpoints. This is part of Google’s A2A protocol for cross-platform agent communication. Our presence here ensures that any agent using Google’s Agent2Agent standard could learn about our offerings.

MCP Catalogs: We have published our services in MCP (Model-Context Protocol) catalogs as well (41 services are exposed via MCP). This ties into the Cloudflare Agents SDK and MCP server ecosystem, meaning agents built on Cloudflare’s stack or others using the Model Context Protocol can fetch our tool definitions (some agent frameworks use MCP servers to list available tools or APIs). Being in these catalogs increases visibility particularly among the “Agents-as-a-Service” platforms and developer sandboxes that emerged after Cloudflare’s integration of x402.

GitHub and documentation references: We have seeded references in open-source code. A search across GitHub shows 170+ references to our x402 endpoints in READMEs or code examples. This likely comes from sample projects, hackathon submissions, or integration guides that mention our platform. Such organic SEO helps crawling bots (and agent evaluators) discover us. Indeed, crawling and SEO-style discovery is a major source of traffic today – around 92% of our ~140 daily requests are from evaluators, bots, or automated crawlers hitting the endpoints (often just to see the 402 response) rather than actual agent end-users. So our indexing for machine discovery is quite decent.

Other channels (missed so far): We currently have no meaningful presence on Farcaster or Frames (decentralized social/agent platforms) – our services are not indexed in those ecosystems yet. We also have no real engagement on developer Twitter/X circles (no one in the agent dev community is talking about us on social media), and minimal traction in Discord agent communities (we set up a bot account, but haven’t fostered a community around it). These are gaps where agent developers share knowledge; our absence means less word-of-mouth or peer recommendation traffic. Similarly, no integration on Dialect (Solana’s messaging/agent platform) and other niche agent networks.

Conversion – turning discovery into payment: Here is the stark reality: discoverability has not translated into economic activity. Despite over 1,200 agent instances encountering our services (as evidenced by unique user-agent strings or keys that have hit our endpoints), we have seen effectively 0 conversions to paid usage from external agents. Our total completed payments are 54 (all from internal testing or demos by us), totaling only ~$47.67 USDC in revenue. We have had 5 agents configure wallets (likely also our own test agents or close partners), but none proceeded to pay beyond test transactions. The conversion rate is 0% – none of the ostensibly interested agents (crawlers, etc.) actually followed through with a payment after receiving the 402 Payment Required response.

Every one of our x402-gated endpoints returns a standardized 402 response when accessed without payment. This includes machine-readable instructions in HTTP headers (X-Payment-Required, X-Payment-Price, etc.) and in the JSON body (pricing, recipient address, etc.), as well as example code (Node.js, Python, cURL snippets) illustrating how to retry with payment. We designed this “402 challenge” flow to be as developer-friendly as possible, essentially serving as on-the-fly documentation for how to pay. For two of our services (e.g. a gas price oracle and a token metadata API), we even allow the first call free – if an agent requests without payment, we actually fulfill one free response (with a header indicating payment was 0 for this trial) to entice them. Despite these measures, we have not yet seen a single agent come back with a valid payment header in a follow-up request. The lack of conversion suggests that agents discovering us are either not truly capable of handling payments or not sufficiently motivated to pay for what we offer.

Likely reasons for low conversion:

Many “agents” hitting us are likely search bots, indexers, or evaluators without wallets (e.g. someone’s agent scanning Bazaar listings, or an AI evaluating available tools but not actually authorized to spend). These will never pay; they just catalog the info.

Some agents may be interested but lack immediate funding or wallet setup. An agent might encounter our 402 and not have a USDC balance handy, or not have the Coinbase facilitator configured, so it moves on.

No prompt-level integration: Unlike human users who see a paywall and might consider paying, an autonomous agent will only pay if its programming includes logic to handle a 402. If the agent’s framework didn’t anticipate needing to pay, it might just treat our 402 as a dead-end. (Today, not all agent frameworks automatically handle payment negotiation – it often requires the developer to specifically include that capability.)

Lack of trust or perceived necessity: The agent (or its owner) might not trust the value or may think it can find a free alternative. With no strong reputation or guarantees from our side, an agent might be programmed to avoid paid services unless absolutely necessary.

In summary, we have succeeded in being visible to agents, but not in convincing the right agents to transact. The challenge now is finding and targeting those agents that can and want to pay for external services – and ensuring they know the value we provide.

4. Friction and Trust Factors

To convert capable agents into customers, we must address any friction points and trust signals that influence an agent (or its developer) when deciding to pay:

Funding friction (pre-funding requirement): Currently an agent (or its owner) must pre-fund a crypto wallet with USDC to use our services. There is no “free trial” beyond the first-call-free on a couple endpoints, and we don’t support credit-based billing by default. This means a developer has to consciously load perhaps a few dollars of USDC on Base into their agent’s wallet ahead of time. We do have a just-in-time payment flow via Coinbase’s CDP facilitator, which can pull funds from a user’s Coinbase account on demand, but that still requires the user to have a Coinbase account linked and authorized. We even set up a “free wallet” tier (a special facilitator endpoint /x402/wallet/free) that would grant limited credits to agents – yet uptake has been zero, indicating it’s either not known or not appealing. The need to manage funds is a point of friction; by contrast, developers are used to free API keys or monthly invoicing in traditional services.

Machine-readable pricing and terms: On the positive side, our pricing is fully transparent and machine-readable. Every 402 response clearly states the price in both human and micro-unit terms (e.g. X-PAYMENT-PRICE: 0.10 and X-PAYMENT-PRICE-MICRO: 100000 for $0.10, given 6 decimal places for USDC) and the currency (USDC). The JSON body also includes a full pricing catalog. This makes it easy for an agent to decide if it can afford the call. We’ve effectively embedded a mini OpenAPI spec in the 402 response to reduce uncertainty. This is in line with x402 protocol’s goal of making negotiation automated and simple.

Lack of SLA or guarantees: One area we have not formalized is any Service Level Agreement (SLA) or performance guarantee for our services. Agents (or their developers) might be wary of paying if they aren’t sure about uptime, latency, or data quality. While we have service descriptions, we haven’t published reliability stats or guarantees. In critical use cases, an agent might stick to known, stable providers (even if slower or subscription-based) rather than an unproven pay-per-call service. Establishing more trust – e.g. via published uptime, audits, or endorsements – could reassure potential users that they get value for their money.

Security and trust signals: Since agents operate autonomously, security is crucial. An agent will only call external services if it trusts that the responses won’t be malicious or erroneous. We need to convey trust signals: for instance, being recognized on x402scan (the explorer for x402 transactions) as a reputable provider, or gaining a certification/trusted badge in discovery registries. Currently, x402scan does list us, but due to no external transactions, our profile there is effectively empty (no activity to show) – which doesn’t inspire confidence. We might consider intentionally funding some activity or getting a “verified provider” stamp if such exists in the ecosystem.

Payment UX for semi-autonomous agents: If an agent is semi-autonomous (requires user approval per spend), the user experience matters. Our 402 includes example code, but do we provide an easy “click to pay” mechanism for a human? In agent contexts, perhaps less relevant, but for dev testing it might help if we had a web dashboard where a dev could simulate and approve a payment for their agent’s request easily. Currently, it’s all CLI or code-driven.

In summary, the core protocol mechanics are smooth (the 402 handshake is automatic and stateless), but external factors – funding, confidence, and ease of integration – may be holding back agents from paying. Reducing any unnecessary friction (like offering limited free credits or integrating with agent platforms’ preferred payment methods) and bolstering our trustworthiness (through reliability and visibility) will be critical.

5. Willingness to Adapt (What We Can Change)

To attract the right agents, we are prepared to adjust our offerings and model – within certain limits – to better align with agent needs:

Agent-specific endpoints or pricing: We can create dedicated endpoints tailored for agent use (we already have 44 such machine-oriented services live). If packaging some services together or offering higher-value data in one call makes it more enticing for agents, we’ll do it. We are also willing to adjust pricing (including raising prices in some cases) to ensure we get serious, value-focused usage rather than spam or frivolous calls. Higher prices might actually signal quality and filter out low-intent traffic, focusing on agents with real ROI behind their calls.

Removing free tiers if needed: Currently only two services allow a free first call. We are open to removing free access entirely if it’s abused or if we decide to enforce commitment – although so far the free calls haven’t been exploited (and they might help onboarding). This will be evaluated based on whether “free” is attracting non-paying noise or genuinely helping conversion.

Bundling and subscriptions: While not implemented yet, we are open to offering “agent plans” or bundles. For example, an agent developer might pre-pay $50 and get a package of calls (like a subscription or bulk discount), avoiding the need for per-call payments each time. This could be done via subscription NFTs or API keys that bypass 402 for authorized agents. It slightly goes against x402’s pure pay-per-call ethos, but if it lowers adoption barriers, it’s on the table.

White-labeling for platforms: We’re willing to white-label our services for agent platforms. If a major agent platform (say, Virtuals or an enterprise vendor) wants to offer our capabilities under their own brand or integrated tightly with their agent framework, we would consider licensing or custom integrations. This might mean the end-user agent doesn’t even know our platform is behind the scenes – they just see a new capability in their environment that “costs” something (perhaps even priced in a different token or credit system). The key for us is that it opens access to a large user base, even if it’s behind another platform’s interface.

Geographical/jurisdictional limits: We will not compromise on legal compliance. We won’t engage with sanctioned jurisdictions or do anything that could classify us as an illegal money transmitter. Our model is non-custodial (we never hold user funds – payments are peer-to-peer via stablecoin), and we intend to keep it that way to avoid regulatory pitfalls. So, for instance, we wouldn’t hold an agent’s funds in escrow or provide custodial accounts – agents must hold their own keys. We also are cautious about any feature that could be seen as creating an “account” or balance with us; we prefer on-demand settlement to remain clearly non-custodial.

Maintaining trust principles: We will not sacrifice core trust/security. For example, if a platform asked us to weaken our security or serve a closed source model without transparency, we’d refuse. User/agent trust comes from us being neutral and reliable.

In short, we are flexible on product and business model aspects (packaging, pricing, integration methods) to meet agents where they are. But we will not cross lines that compromise security, decentralization ethos, or legal compliance. This balanced approach should allow us to tailor our strategy for different agent communities as needed.

6. Success Criteria and Current Baseline

How do we define success with paying agents? Our 90-day targets to gauge if our strategy is working are ambitious but concrete:

At least 10 distinct paying agents per day – up from effectively 0 today. This would indicate that we have multiple independent agents regularly using (and paying for) our services.

$5,000+ in monthly recurring revenue (MRR) from agent payments – a significant jump from <$50 total so far. Hitting this would prove that agents find enough value to spend real budgets here.

Partnerships or integrations with marquee agent platforms – e.g. being included in the “default agent stack” of one major framework or securing a named partnership. A win would be an announcement like “Virtuals Protocol integrates Coin Railz services for its AI agents” or ai16z/Eliza using our platform as a recommended paid tool. Even better if we get into Coinbase’s official AgentKit/x402 developer showcase or reference apps.

Recognition in the ecosystem metrics: For instance, x402scan (the explorer for x402 activity) should show our services with regular traffic, and perhaps label us as a trending provider. We’d like to go from an effectively invisible profile to a notable one on such dashboards.

These goals tie back to our core business: more paying agents means real usage and revenue, partnerships signal trust and reach, and ecosystem recognition feeds a virtuous cycle of further discovery.

Success for us means tapping into this emerging agent economy and capturing our fair share of those pay-per-use interactions.

7. Strategy to Reach Pay-Capable Agents

To turn things around, we need to actively target the agents that have the ability and willingness to pay, rather than expending effort on agents stuck in closed loops or unaware of external tools. Here are our key strategic actions:

7.1 Focus on Open Agent Ecosystems and Platforms

Agents that can pay us will be those operating in environments that support external calls and on-chain payments. We should prioritize integration and presence in these ecosystems:

Farcaster and decentralized social agents: Farcaster (the decentralized social network) is evolving into a hub for AI agents, largely because every Farcaster user/agent inherently has an Ethereum L2 wallet attached. Projects like Neynar have already enabled x402 payments within Farcaster mini-apps. We should ensure our services are known and easily accessible to Farcaster-based agents. This could mean publishing Farcaster “frames” or mini-apps that showcase our APIs (so any agent on Farcaster can use them), or collaborating with teams like Neynar to feature our endpoints as examples. Farcaster agents can pay (the wallet is there by default), so it’s about giving them a reason and easy way to use that capability with our services.

Cloudflare Workers/Agents & MCP: Cloudflare’s Agents SDK and MCP server infrastructure explicitly added support for x402. Developers building agents on Cloudflare (which ties into the broader Model Context Protocol) can integrate paid tools. We already list our services in the MCP catalog; now we should engage with Cloudflare’s developer community. For example, writing a tutorial on “Using Coin Railz services with Cloudflare Agents SDK” or contributing an example to their x402 playground could put us in front of these developers. Since Cloudflare is championing agentic payments (even proposing deferred payment schemes for agents), aligning with them can bring us serious users who are experimenting with paid agent models.

Cross-chain agent frameworks (A2A/AP2): Google’s Agent2Agent (A2A) protocol with the Agentic Payments Protocol (AP2) extension is one avenue through which agents across platforms communicate and pay each other. Coinbase’s x402 is integrated as a module there, and our services are already in the A2A registry. We should remain compatible and visible in any A2A developer tools. If Google Cloud is showcasing demos (like the Lowe’s demo), we might try to get involved or at least ensure our APIs meet any standards to be easily consumed in such cross-agent workflows.

LangChain/AutoGPT and similar agent frameworks: Many indie devs use frameworks like LangChain to give agents tool-use abilities. We can create LangChain tool wrappers for our paid APIs – essentially plugins that handle the 402 flow under the hood. By open-sourcing a wrapper, any LangChain-based agent could simply import our tool and have payment integrated (provided they supply a wallet key). This reduces the barrier for those devs to try our service in their agent’s toolset. Similarly, for popular autonomous agent projects (AutoGPT, BabyAGI variants), we could contribute an extension that allows using x402 tools. This reaches the hacker segment effectively.

Target “paying agent” concentrations: Look for events or hackathons where agent payment is a theme. For example, if there’s an x402 hackathon or an AI agent challenge sponsored by Coinbase or others, we should be present (sponsor, provide free credits, or have our services as recommended tools). The developers who participate are exactly those trying to build agents that handle money, hence likely to appreciate our offerings.

7.2 Engage Communities and Evangelize

Right now, we are present in registries but not truly part of any community conversation. We need to change that:

Developer community presence: Establish an official presence in key forums – e.g. a Discord server (or join existing ones like the Virtuals Discord or agent-dev Discords) where we can interact with agent developers. We can share what we’ve built, ask for feedback, and highlight use cases. If developers frequently see us discussing agent payment solutions, they’ll remember us when they need one. Twitter (X) is also a place where many AI + crypto developers share ideas. We should start posting interesting content (demos, short clips of an agent using our API to accomplish something neat, etc.) to generate buzz. Being entirely absent from these conversations has made us effectively invisible except to crawling bots.

Showcase success stories: We might need to create the first success story ourselves. For example, build or collaborate on an autonomous trading agent that uses our platform and demonstrably earns profit or performs better by paying for data. If we can show an agent that made $X by spending $Y on our services (where $Y << $X), that’s a compelling case for others. This kind of case study can be shared in blog posts, at meetups, and on social media. It provides social proof that paying per request can be worthwhile.

Educational content: Many potential users might simply not understand how to implement an agent that pays. We should write guides/tutorials that lower the learning curve. E.g., “Monetize your AI agent in 5 steps with x402” or “How to give your agent a wallet and let it pay for API calls”. By educating the community, we indirectly promote our platform (as a featured use case in those guides). The Medium article about swarms and x402 or the Vercel blog on x402 are good examples we can follow to produce content.

Partnership outreach: Directly approach the teams behind Virtuals Protocol and ai16z’s Eliza. Even if their current focus is on their own token ecosystems (e.g., ai16z planning A2A transactions in their native token, or Virtuals potentially using SOL or another coin), they might still see value in connecting to the broader x402 world. Perhaps propose a pilot where Eliza agents can use a stablecoin bridge to access external services like ours that aren’t available internally. If we position it not as competition but as complementary (their agents become more powerful with access to outside info), we might secure an integration. A partnership could involve us adapting our pricing to accept their native token via a swap or supporting their authentication. Even a white-label offering could be on the table – e.g. “Eliza Premium Tools powered by Coin Railz” where payments are abstracted away but ultimately settle in stablecoin to us. The key is to open a dialogue; these communities are large (Eliza has thousands of agents live) and could unleash many paying users if we plug in.

Leverage Coinbase and x402 Foundation connections: Since our solution is built on Coinbase’s x402, we should participate in the x402 Foundation initiatives (Cloudflare’s blog mentioned a foundation to drive adoption). This might include joining working groups, speaking at webinars, or contributing to the standard. By being active here, we’ll be seen by other builders in the space and can subtly promote that “hey, we have 100+ services ready for your agents to use”. If Coinbase or Cloudflare highlights us as an early adopter success story, that could dramatically boost our credibility.

7.3 Reduce Friction for Onboarding Paying Agents

Even if we reach the right audience, we must make it effortless for them to start paying us:

Offer initial credits or easy trials: Instead of expecting an agent dev to go buy USDC and fund a wallet, we could offer a small startup grant for new agents. For example, we generate a one-time coupon (in the form of a signed permit) that allows an agent $1 worth of free calls. This could be distributed to developers at hackathons or via an application form. The cost to us is low and it lets them experience the flow risk-free. Given no one used our /wallet/free API, we might need a more proactive approach like this.

Support multiple stablecoins or currencies: If some agents operate on chains where USDC (Base) isn’t their main currency, consider supporting what they use. For instance, an agent on Solana might have USDC-SPL or SOL. We currently emphasize Base USDC, but x402 is chain-agnostic. If we enabled a popular alternative (say USDC on Solana, or EUROC for European devs), we might attract those who hesitated because they didn’t want to bridge funds to Base. We should of course weigh this against splitting liquidity, but being flexible could net a few conversions.

Build trust with reliability: As we start getting real users, prioritize uptime and speed so that first experiences are good. Publish a status page or real-time stats of our service performance. Agents will likely share feedback in their circles – a positive first experience (“the API was fast and never failed”) will encourage others, while a negative one (“my agent paid but got no response”) would be disastrous to word-of-mouth. We might not formally issue an SLA yet, but we can at least publicly commit to certain standards (e.g. 99.9% uptime, typical latency <1s) to give assurances.

Feedback loops: Provide an easy way for developers to get support. If an agent tries to pay and something goes wrong, the developer should quickly get help. Setting up a Discord or forum for support can turn frustrated users into happy ones before they give up. It’s part of friction reduction: knowing help is available makes one more willing to try a new tech.

7.4 Align with Agent Incentives and Economics

Understanding why an agent (or its owner) would pay is crucial. We should ensure our value proposition is crystal clear and maybe even adjust how we charge to align with agent incentives:

Highlight cost-benefit: Emphasize use cases where paying per request is cheaper or more efficient than alternatives. For example, if an agent only needs a specific piece of data occasionally, paying $0.10 on demand is better than a $100/month data subscription. Or an agent can save time (and thus money) by outsourcing a task to our specialized service for a small fee rather than using a lot of its own compute. Quantify these benefits in our marketing.

Outcome-based pricing (future idea): Agents care about outcomes (e.g. profitable trades, completed tasks). If feasible, we could experiment with success-based fees – say an agent pays a tiny fee for data but a larger fee only if it leads to a profitable action. This might be complex to implement, but thinking in this direction could differentiate us. At minimum, ensure our pricing “makes sense” in context (if we provide trading signals, perhaps charge a fraction of the expected profit from a good trade, etc.).

Agent budgets and spend management: Provide tools for users to manage their agent’s spending on our platform. For instance, a dashboard showing how much their agent spent this week and on what. This helps build trust that costs won’t run away. Agents already can enforce budgets on-chain (e.g. session keys with spend limits); we should integrate smoothly into those paradigms by, say, accepting a pre-signed spending permit that an agent’s policy system generates. By respecting agent-side budgeting tools, we become a friendly option in their stack.

7.5 Watch and Adapt to the “Self-Enclosed” Agent Platforms

Not all agents are free to roam the open web. Some live in self-contained agent platforms (with their own networks and currencies). Examples include:

ai16z/Eliza and Virtuals Protocol: These frameworks are moving toward making their own ecosystems (even an “AI L1 blockchain” for ai16z Eliza). They’re introducing their native tokens for agent-to-agent transactions and service marketplaces. Agents in those environments might prefer or be required to use the native token (e.g., ai16z token or $KITE on Kite network) for payments. This is a challenge for us since our system currently expects USDC. While these are “closed” in a sense, we should not ignore them – they have vibrant agent communities. Our strategy here: integrate if you can’t beat. If Virtuals or Eliza agents can’t easily use USDC, explore bridging options. Perhaps work with those teams to allow our services to be offered inside their “app store” with automatic conversion (the platform could convert their token to USDC in the background via a facilitator). We must tread carefully (to avoid being a custodian or exchange ourselves), but maybe the platform can handle conversion and just call our API with USDC. If they are not open to that, consider running a node within their platform – e.g. deploy a version of our service on their chain (if technically feasible) that accepts their token. That essentially would be a white-label or mirror of our service inside their garden. It’s not our main focus (since it’s a lot of work per platform), but if one of these ecosystems dominates agent usage, integration could unlock many paying users we’d otherwise miss.

Kite and other “AI Chains”: As seen with Kite’s approach, the trend is some platforms want agents to use their native coin for everything (gas and payments). Our plan should monitor these developments. If one network’s agents start to thrive, we could adapt by deploying our services as smart contracts or APIs on that network (charging their coin). But this may conflict with our principle of not custody and regulatory caution. At the very least, we can ensure our content (the data or analysis we provide) is unique enough that even those closed platforms might partner rather than replicate it. If we have proprietary analytics, they may prefer to pay us (even in a roundabout way) than to build it themselves.

7.6 Measure, Learn, and Iterate

As we implement these strategies, we will closely measure outcomes and be ready to adjust:

Track metrics like number of 402 responses followed by a payment (conversion funnel) to see if improvements like better documentation or community outreach correlate with actual payments.

Gather feedback from any new paying agent – even if it’s just one or two initially, do a deep dive with those developers. Why did they choose to pay? What almost stopped them? This qualitative insight can guide further tweaks (maybe it turns out one particular service is the killer app that others would pay for if they knew about it).

A/B test pricing or free call limits: Perhaps allow one group of agents a free trial vs another not, and see which yields more paying behavior subsequently.

Remain agile regarding which communities to focus on. If, say, Farcaster experiments aren’t yielding paying users but a new platform (hypothetically, maybe “Mastercard Agent Pay” as hinted by a16z) emerges where agents do pay, be ready to pivot attention there.

In essence, our approach is both proactive and adaptive: go where the paying agents are (or soon will be), make it enticing and easy for them to use our services, and remove any barriers in their way.

Conclusion: By implementing the above strategy, we aim to transform our platform from a purely technically integrated but economically idle service into a thriving component of the agent economy. The pieces are in place – a unique value proposition of on-demand services, a cutting-edge payment protocol, and an ecosystem hungry for agent capabilities. We now need to execute on outreach and integration so that the agents who have “wallets and will to pay” actually choose to spend their money on our APIs. If successful, we’ll not only hit our 90-day targets but also establish a long-term foothold as a default resource in the toolbelts of autonomous agents across platforms. The era of agents paying agents is dawning, and we want to be at the center of this new machine economy – earning our keep one 402 payment at a time.
